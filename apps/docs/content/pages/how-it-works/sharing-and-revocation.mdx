---
title: Sharing & revocation
description: How you share a single note with another person, how recipient keys are verified, and why revocation is forward-only.
---

Sharing in NoteKit is **per item** — you grant one note or task to one person, not your whole vault.
It reuses the same age multi-recipient mechanism as everything else.

## The key directory

To share with someone, NoteKit needs their public keys. Users publish theirs to a **directory** the
server hosts at `/directory/keys`. The directory stores **public keys only** — each person's recovery
signing key plus their signed device records.

Crucially, the server is **untrusted** here. When you look someone up, your client **verifies every
device record's signature** against that person's published recovery signing key before it will
encrypt anything to them. A server that returns a forged device record can't get you to encrypt to an
attacker's key — the unverified recipient is dropped.

You can also compare **safety numbers** out of band (same mechanism as
[pairing](/how-it-works/devices-and-trust#safety-numbers-mitm-detection)) to confirm you looked up the
right person.

## Sharing an item

<Mermaid chart={`
sequenceDiagram
  participant You as You
  participant Dir as Key directory
  participant Vault as Git vault
  You->>Dir: look up recipient by email
  Dir-->>You: recovery signing key + signed device records
  You->>You: verify every device record's signature
  You->>Vault: append grant to .notekit/shares/{kind}-{id}.json
  You->>Vault: re-encrypt the item to [your recipients + their verified keys]
`} />

- The grant is recorded in a cleartext manifest `.notekit/shares/{kind}-{id}.json` — it lists who the
  item is shared with (email, their signing key, their device recipients).
- The item itself is **re-encrypted** to the widened recipient set, so their devices can now decrypt
  that one item. Nothing else in your vault is affected.

<Callout type="warn">
The share manifest is cleartext metadata: the server can see **which items are shared with whom**
(by email). The item **contents** stay encrypted. This is a conscious trade-off — see the
[threat model](/how-it-works/threat-model).
</Callout>

## Forward-only revocation

Un-sharing removes the grant from the manifest and re-encrypts the item **forward-only**. The same is
true when you revoke a device (which additionally rotates the vault key).

"Forward-only" means exactly what it says, and it's a property of Git, not a weakness to paper over:

- New writes are encrypted to the **reduced** recipient set — the removed party can't read anything
  new.
- **Git history still contains** the old ciphertext, which was encrypted to the old set. Anyone who
  already had access (and kept a clone) can still read what they could already read.

In other words, revocation stops **future** access; it cannot un-send what was already shared. This is
the honest, industry-standard guarantee — trying to "delete" already-disclosed data would be security
theater. If content is truly sensitive after a revocation, rotate the secret itself (e.g. change the
password you stored), don't rely on retroactively hiding history.

## Least-privilege agents

Agents (CLI/MCP) are just devices with their own keys, and by default they can **write** but only
**read what has been granted to them**. When you approve an agent, the vault key is re-wrapped to
include it (or specific items are resealed to it) — it decrypts with its **own** key, never your master
phrase. See [MCP for agents](/guides/connect-an-agent) for the agent surface.
